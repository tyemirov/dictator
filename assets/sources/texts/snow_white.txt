Once upon a time, a queen sat at her window stitching by the snow-white light of a winter afternoon. As she worked, she pricked her finger, and three drops of blood fell onto the pure snow outside. She thought, “If only I had a daughter as white as snow, as red as blood, and with hair as dark as ebony.” Not long after, the queen gave birth to a little girl with skin like fresh snow, lips as red as a rose, and hair black as midnight. They named her Snow White. Joy filled the castle—but sorrow came soon, for the queen grew ill and passed away before the child’s first birthday.

When Snow White was still very young, the king remarried. Her new stepmother was beautiful but proud and cruel. She ruled the household with strict commands and demanded that everyone praise her above all else. On the wall of her private chamber hung an enchanted mirror, framed in gold, in which she saw her own reflection and asked each day, “Magic mirror on the wall, who in this land is fairest of all?” And the mirror always answered, “You, my queen, are fairest of all.”

Snow White grew into a kind and gentle girl. She helped servants carry water, fed the palace birds, and sang soft melodies as she cleaned. Every time she passed the stepmother’s chamber, she heard the question and the same answer. But on Snow White’s seventh birthday, the mirror’s reply changed. When the queen asked, “Magic mirror on the wall, who in this land is fairest of all?” the glass voice said, “Queen, you are fair; it is true. But Snow White is a thousand times fairer than you.” Jealousy and rage filled the queen’s heart.

The queen summoned her huntsman and ordered, “Take Snow White into the forest. Let no trace of her be found again.” She gave him a dagger and her cruel command. The huntsman led Snow White to the shadowy woods. The girl, trusting and innocent, walked beside him, humming a gentle tune about spring flowers. But when they reached a clearing, the huntsman could not strike. He remembered Snow White’s laughter and saw tears in her eyes. Instead of obeying the queen, he cut a branch from a pine tree and told Snow White to flee at once, hiding deeper in the forest.

Snow White ran until her legs ached. She left the path and wept beneath tall pines whose branches whispered overhead. Soon she came upon a small cottage, its windows low and its walls covered in moss. Tired and hungry, she entered. Inside, everything was tiny: seven chairs, seven plates, and seven tiny beds. Finding no one at home, she dared to sit in one chair. It was perfect. Then she tasted a bit of porridge from a small bowl and found it sweet and warm. Finally, she lay down on one of the little beds and fell into a gentle sleep.

Late in the afternoon, seven dwarfs came home from the mines, carrying pickaxes and lamps. They found their cottage tidier than ever. “Who has been sitting in my chair?” one cried. “Who has eaten my porridge?” another asked. And each dwarf recognized his own bowl, chair, and bed. But when they saw Snow White asleep, they stood silent in wonder. “She is as lovely as the dawn,” said the eldest dwarf kindly. They let her sleep until evening, then woke her gently and asked her story.

Snow White told them how her stepmother had tried to kill her and how the huntsman had saved her life. “You may stay with us,” they said. “You can cook and clean, and we will protect you.” Grateful, she agreed. Each morning she swept and dusted the cottage, set out their food, and mended their clothes. In return, the dwarfs warned her never to open the door to strangers while they worked in the mine each day.

Back at the palace, the queen again consulted her mirror. When she asked, “Magic mirror on the wall, who is fairest now?” it answered, “Queen, you are fair; it is true. But Snow White lives and surpasses you.” Furious, the queen disguised herself as an old peddler and took a basket of lace and ribbons to the dwarfs’ cottage. Snow White, eager for pretty things, unbarred the door. The queen offered her a red bodice with silver laces. As Snow White tried it on, the cruel queen tightened the laces until the girl could barely breathe, then slipped away. Snow White fainted; when the dwarfs returned, they found her near death. They loosened the laces and revived her with tears and gentle words.

The queen, enraged that her plan had failed, crafted a second disguise: a comb merchant. She knocked on the cottage door, holding a beautiful comb carved of ivory. “This will make your hair shine,” she said. Snow White, touched by its beauty, allowed her in. As soon as the comb touched her hair, the queen pricked her finger on a sharp edge, and Snow White fell unconscious. But the dwarfs, coming home, saw the wicked device and shook it free. With much sorrow and many tears, they rescued Snow White again.

Still seething with hatred, the queen prepared one final trick. She mixed a bright red apple with a drop of deadly poison. Dressed once more as a fruit vendor, she came to the cottage and cried, “Apples, apples! Sweet, crisp apples!” Snow White, hungry for a treat, took a bite—and collapsed at once, as though she were dead. The queen hurried home, certain her enemy was gone.

When the dwarfs returned, they found Snow White lying pale and still. They could not revive her. They placed her in a glass coffin, lining it with soft pillows and wreaths of flowers. The coffin stood on a green hill near the edge of the forest, where the sun shone through the trees and birds sang overhead. Though they mourned, the dwarfs kept watch day and night, for they loved her gentle heart.

One day, a traveling prince happened by. He had heard of a beautiful princess sleeping in a glass coffin. When he saw Snow White, his breath caught in his throat. He knelt beside her and wept, moved by her beauty and the sadness of her fate. “Take me with you,” he whispered to his attendants. “Let me carry her home to my castle, so she may rest in a place of honor.”

The dwarfs agreed, and the coffin was lifted onto the prince’s white horse. As the bearers carried the coffin down a narrow trail, they stumbled over a root. The jar loosened the poisoned piece of apple in Snow White’s throat, and she coughed once, opening her eyes. The prince, stunned with joy, reached for her hand. She rose slowly, brushing leaves from her hair. “Where am I?” she asked in a soft voice.

The prince smiled. “You are safe now,” he said. He led her to his castle, where music and laughter greeted them. Snow White met the prince’s kind parents and all the noble guests. Dressed in a gown of pale blue and silver, she walked beside the prince through glittering halls and sunlit gardens.

Soon a grand wedding was announced. Guests came from every corner of the kingdom: kings, queens, and common folk alike. Even the dwarfs were invited, and they stood proudly at the edge of the celebrations, their eyes bright with happiness. The wedding feast lasted three whole days, with music, dancing, and feasting under canopies of flowers.

Far away, the wicked stepmother watched in her mirror. When the glass voice told her that Snow White was alive and queen, her rage consumed her. She rushed to the wedding, disguised in a dark cloak, hoping to harm Snow White once more. But the king’s guards seized her at the gate. As punishment for her cruelty, she was forced to wear red-hot iron shoes and dance until she could dance no more.

Peace returned to the land. Snow White and the prince ruled wisely and kindly, caring for their people and protecting the forests where the dwarfs still mined. Every morning, they visited the glass coffin on the hill to give thanks for her second chance at life. And the dwarfs, who had saved her so often, were honored guests at every feast and festival.

Snow White never forgot the little cottage in the woods or the seven friends who loved her. She understood that true beauty comes from a pure heart and that kindness can break even the darkest magic. And so they all lived happily ever after, under skies of dawn-pink and gold, in a kingdom where goodness always triumphed.
